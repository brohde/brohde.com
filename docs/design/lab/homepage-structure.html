homepage structure: three treatments + the article page

Brian's critique: "everything above the chat kind of just flows together, hard to see the pieces." His open question: keep it terminal-like, or make the page more standalone and keep the terminal for the chat experience? Mid-review addition: the homepage and posts become "designed" surfaces, candidate face LL Circular Pro, while the chat serves content. Three treatments below, real copy, real tokens, both grounds, plus the article page in the two designed directions. All links and buttons in the stages are inert. Circular is embedded from Brian's licensed woffs for review only; production is gated on the Lineto web license (#20).

A · structured terminal (the control)

Same fiction, real hierarchy. The blur has one root cause: six pieces share one margin, one size, one color temperature, and two of them (profile links, command menu) share the exact same bracket affordance. Three moves fix it without touching the concept:

  • A spacing scale. Tight inside a group (2 to 8px), 44px between groups. The page becomes three objects: identity, console, log. Clients scan in that order: who is this, what can I do here, where is the proof.
  • One affordance per link type. Brackets now mean exactly one thing: a command that runs here. Outbound profile links lose their brackets and become underlined links with a dim outbound arrow. The old page had two identical-looking bracket rows doing different jobs; that sameness is most of the blur.
  • The console is docked as one object. Command chips, the input, and the usage comment share a single bordered well. The chips sit where autocomplete suggestions already appear (#48), so the menu reads as "suggestions, surfaced by default." The log gets a # build.log section label with a hairline. The prompt was already the only bordered object on the page; docking the menu into it makes the border mean something.

Indentation was considered and rejected: terminals left-align output, and the grouping already reads from rhythm alone. No Circular here by design; A is the control that shows how far spacing and affordance discipline go on system fonts alone.

B · standalone identity + terminal chat object (Circular)

The split Brian floated, taken literally. One seam rule governs everything: Circular wherever a client reads, mono wherever the terminal is real (the prompt, command chips, comments, timestamps, the status line).

  • Identity block goes editorial. Wordmark in Circular Bold at 62px, ink not mint, lowercase kept as the wordmark's own style; bio rewritten as two sentence-case lines at reading sizes. Hiring managers and clients get a confident, credible masthead in the face Brian's own resume uses.
  • The terminal concentrates into one object. A chat card with its own chrome: a title bar (brohde.com · chat, status dot), chips, the input, the usage comment. Everything inside is mono on the well ground. The card is the one place the fiction lives, so it stops competing with the identity for the same visual register.
  • The log leans editorial, deliberately. Titles in Circular Medium at 19px carry the scan; the bracketed mono datestamps stay as the terminal's thread through the designed surface. Brian already noticed the sans titles read best; this promotes them to the primary voice of the list.
  • The caret survives as the bridge. A mint block caret rests after the Circular wordmark (single-cursor model, #63): the page still has a focus, the design still blinks.

Risk to name honestly: with a soft well-toned card, the page can drift toward "nice portfolio with a search box." The card chrome (title bar, dot, hairlines) is what keeps it reading as an instrument; do not soften it further.

C · the phosphor slab (the terminal is a screen, not a theme)

The studio's third take. When "the pieces blur together," the deepest fix is to stop painting the whole page in the terminal's colors and make the terminal a physical object on the page: one slab of always-night phosphor glass (fixed #0B110E ground, mint, scanlines, a faint glow halo) holding everything interactive. The page around it is editorial Circular and follows the system ground.

  • The seam becomes physical, not stylistic. In light mode the slab reads as a CRT sitting on paper; in night mode, a screen glowing in a dark room, separated by its border and halo rather than by ground contrast. A screen is dark even in a bright room; the metaphor answers the light-mode question for free.
  • Chat expands the slab. Typing or clicking grows the slab to the full viewport, which makes the alternate-screen chat (#24) literal: you lean into the screen. The chat screen is always night, one consistent phosphor identity everywhere the LLM speaks.
  • The log becomes a ledger. Unbracketed mono dates in a fixed column, Circular titles, one hairline per row. Reads as a work record, which is what a client is here to audit; the fixed date column also gives the eye a rail the current layout lacks.
  • Identity is compact. Smaller than B's masthead, because the slab is the distinctive object and two heroes on one page would re-create the original competition problem.

Measured on the slab's fixed ground (both site grounds, since it never changes): mint 12.42:1, dim 7.41:1, ink 14.63:1. The slab's separation from the night page ground is decorative (border + halo), not contrast-borne, so nothing fails when the grounds nearly match.

[2026-02-12] · build.log · eight angle

Eight Angle: I built a 47-class yoga pose classifier that runs entirely on the phone

47pose classes, on-device
92.2%cross-validated accuracy
~20fpslive inference on the phone

Eight Angle is an iOS app that watches your yoga practice through the front camera, on-device, and tells you what pose you're in. It records the session, builds a timeline of what you practiced, and turns that into insights and achievements afterward.

None of that works if pose detection happens on a server. Yoga is practiced at home, badly lit, six feet from a phone propped against something. Streaming that footage anywhere is a nonstarter, on privacy grounds and on latency grounds. So detection, feature extraction, and classification all have to run on the phone, in real time.

The migration decision

Eight Angle originally ran on Apple's own Vision framework for pose detection. It worked, until I checked how often it actually found a body in the training images: 71.2% of the time. Close to a third of my training data was unusable because Vision couldn't detect the pose at all.

I ran a controlled evaluation against Google's MediaPipe on the same 1,603 images. MediaPipe found 420 more usable poses, pushing detection rate to 97.4% and classification accuracy from 89.6% to 93.4%, with lower variance too. 

$ pose-eval --detector mediapipe --images 1603
detection rate   71.2% → 97.4%    (+420 usable poses)
accuracy         89.6% → 93.4%    (±1.2%)

I documented the one place the migration made things worse, honestly, rather than burying it: headstand accuracy dropped 12.2%. I traced it to five specific misclassified images. Not a detector bug: MediaPipe was surfacing real edge cases in a 41-sample class that Vision had simply never detected in the first place.

verdict · studio recommendation

1 · terminal everywhere, or standalone + terminal chat?

Split, with a physical seam: ship treatment C. The answer to "the pieces blur together" is not more terminal discipline or less terminal fiction; it is deciding what the terminal IS. C's rule is one sentence: the terminal is a screen on the page, not the page's paint. Everything a client reads (identity, titles, articles) is a designed Circular surface in both grounds; everything the machine says or accepts lives on one slab of always-night phosphor glass. That single rule fixes the grouping problem, survives light mode without compromise, gives the boot sequence its most natural stage, and makes the chat expansion (#24) literal instead of thematic. If Brian finds the permanent dark slab too heavy on paper, treatment B is the identical architecture with a ground-following card: same components, softer seam, nothing else changes.

Treatment A is not discarded either way: its three rules (one affordance per link type, a real spacing scale, the console docked as one object) apply inside whatever ships, including the chat screen itself.

2 · Circular or system fonts on the designed surfaces?

Circular, gated on the license (#20). Brian's instinct is right and measurable in this lab: the sans titles already carry the log, and the identity block only becomes a masthead when the face has real weight. Circular Bold's lowercase wordmark also matches his resume, one voice across every client touchpoint. Mono stays system-stack everywhere the terminal is real; we never load a mono webfont.

  • Loading plan (#62): woff2 subsets (~22 to 24KB per weight as cut for this lab), three weights max (Book 400, Medium 500, Bold 700), preload Book + Bold, font-display: swap, size-adjust'd system fallback so the swap is CLS-free. Italics not shipped until a page needs them.
  • If the Lineto web license stalls, treatment B/C still work on the system sans; they lose warmth, not structure. The license blocks polish, not architecture.

3 · the article page

Ship the designed article under the same seam rule. Circular Book 17px / 1.75 at 62ch for body, Circular Bold title, mono dateline, a mono stat band for the headline numbers, inline numbers left in the body face so metric-heavy prose reads as prose. Terminal excerpts set as phosphor glass (the C variant) are the studio's pick: they turn each case study's evidence into terminal output, the same material the visitor already talked to. Hero band stays reserved for the glyph tide (#59) with per-page palette; the placeholder marks its slot until that phase.

5 · constraints audit

  • Boot (#36/#64): compatible in all three; entry-only unchanged. C upgrades it: the slab is the thing that boots.
  • Cursor (#63): the resting block caret survives in all three, after the wordmark; it is the bridge between the designed surface and the machine. Input carets only on focus.
  • Keyboard (#28): every interactive element in every stage is a native link or button; focus-visible ring on the accent everywhere; chips are buttons, entries are links.
  • Contrast, measured (WCAG AA, 4.5:1 body minimum): night ink 14.19:1, night dim 7.19:1 (7.41:1 on the slab), night mint 12.05:1 (12.42:1 on the slab), night dim on well 6.54:1; light ink 16.05:1, light dim 7.97:1, light accent 7.20:1, light dim on well 7.34:1, light accent on well 6.63:1. Worst pairing anywhere is 6.54:1; everything passes with margin. Slab separation in night mode is decorative (border + halo), never contrast-dependent.
  • Dashes: none, anywhere, per #18. Ranges use "to", metadata uses middots, ISO dates use hyphens, metric arrows per VOICE.md.
  • Perf (#62): A is free. B/C add ~70KB of woff2 across three weights, preloaded, swap-rendered; everything else stays static HTML + system mono.

6 · open questions for Brian (via the studio lead)

  • Case on the designed surface: the mocks keep the wordmark lowercase but set bio and article prose in sentence case. Keep that split, or go lowercase everywhere?
  • Lineto web license (#20): confirm before any production Circular; the lab embeds his licensed woffs for review only.
  • C specifically: comfortable with a permanently dark slab on the light ground? It is the concept's spine; if it feels heavy, B is the fallback with zero rework.
  • Article dateline: keep "build.log" as the archive's public name in the dateline, or plain "log"? (#58 says visitor-facing copy avoids "terminal"; "build.log" reads as a product name, not the word terminal, so the studio kept it.)
